🔁 Spec-Driven Chain — define done before you start
What a spec-driven chain is
A normal chain asks "produce the best output for this task." A spec-driven chain asks "produce output that satisfies these specific, measurable conditions — and prove that it does."

The difference is structural: Step 3 is a hostile auditor — a separate AI persona with no loyalty to the Step 2 output, whose only job is to find every way the attempt fails the spec. Step 4 either accepts the output or revises it based on the audit, not based on the original author's confidence in their own work.

This is adjacent to loop engineering. In loop engineering, an autonomous agent runs the same task repeatedly against a verifiable condition until it passes. This is the manual equivalent — you carry each step, but the architecture is the same: define done → attempt → independent audit → revise or accept.

How to write a good success spec:
Weak spec (fails because it's not verifiable)"Write a good analysis of our pricing strategy." → Two people cannot agree on whether "good" was achieved. Strong spec (passes because two people can agree)"Write an analysis that: (1) identifies the three highest-risk assumptions in our current pricing, (2) names at least one competitor price point for each assumption, (3) concludes with a single go/no-go recommendation with a stated confidence level, and (4) contains no claims about market size without citing a source and year." → Two people reading both the spec and the output can independently agree whether each condition was met.
The rule: every condition in your spec must be independently verifiable. "Good," "clear," and "comprehensive" are not verifiable. Counts, named items, explicit conclusions, and cited sources are.

03 · Generated Prompt
🔗 Prompt Chain — linked steps the AI cannot skip or compress
What a prompt chain is
Instead of one large prompt the AI can compress or skip through — a chain is a sequence of focused prompts where each step feeds directly into the next. The AI cannot skip steps because each prompt only works after completing the previous one.

How to run the chain — step by step:
  1. Click Copy Step 1. Paste into Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini. Run it.
  2. Copy the AI's complete response.
  3. Click Copy Step 2. Find the line: [PASTE STEP 1 OUTPUT HERE — replace this entire line]. Replace that whole line with the response you copied. Paste the edited prompt into the AI. Run it.
  4. Repeat: copy each response, paste into the next step's placeholder, run.
  5. Some later steps need multiple previous outputs — the placeholder tells you exactly which ones.
Tips that make a real difference:
  1. Use the same AI session for all steps — the AI keeps context across turns, making later steps more coherent.
  2. Review each output before using it as input. If Step 2 output seems shallow, fix it before pasting into Step 3. Errors compound across steps.
  3. Paste the response into the Capture Response section below to save it and extract good examples for your Expert Layer.
  4. The handoff note below each step tells you exactly what to carry forward.
Why chains produce better output:
Single master promptAI reads all instructions at once. Compresses 4 steps into 1 response. Skips verification steps. Context window mostly consumed by the prompt before the actual task begins. Prompt chainEach step uses a fraction of the context window — more working memory for the actual task. Steps cannot be compressed. You review and correct at each handoff. Later steps build on verified output, not assumptions. Result: 16–17/20 estimated quality vs 13–15 for a single prompt.

🔒 Force a Reasoning Technique +1
What this does: The tool normally asks the AI to pick its own technique. This overrides that — you hard-code exactly which technique to use.

When to use it:
  • You've run this prompt before and know which technique actually worked
  • Your domain has a clear best-fit technique you've tested
  • The AI keeps picking the wrong technique (check the AUDIT line in its first reply to see what it chose)
How to choose the right technique:
  • Your brief is a plan or strategy that could fail → Adversarial / Pre-mortem
  • Your brief has wrong assumptions baked in → First Principles
  • Your brief is vague or you're not sure what you really need → Socratic Drilling
  • Your topic needs complete, exhaustive coverage → MECE Decomposition
  • You know a similar problem was solved in another field → Analogical Reasoning
  • First time running this task? Leave empty and let the AI choose.
✅ Paste a Good Output Example +1.5 (highest single gain)
What this does: Showing the AI a real example of ideal output is more powerful than 500 words of instructions. It teaches format, depth, tone, and structure all at once.

What to paste: A response from any AI — or text you wrote yourself — that hit the right quality for your task. It doesn't need to be on the same topic. The AI learns your format and register, not the specific content.

What to look for in a good example:
  • The right level of depth (not too shallow, not too academic)
  • The structure you want (prose vs bullets, short vs detailed, numbered steps vs flowing analysis)
  • The tone (direct, clinical, conversational, formal)
  • The reasoning style (surface observation vs deep analysis with trade-offs)
Don't have a past example yet? Run the prompt once without this field. Note the best 2–3 paragraphs from the response. Paste those back in here as your good example for the next run. After 2–3 iterations you will have a strong calibration example.

Minimum: 2–3 sentences. Longer is better. Up to ~500 words is ideal.

❌ Paste a Bad Output Example +0.5
What this does: Showing what NOT to do is often more effective than more instructions about what TO do. The AI has default patterns it falls back to — this overrides them.

What to paste: A response that was too generic, too shallow, wrong tone, or missed the point. Add one sentence before the example saying why it was bad.

Example format:
"This response was too generic — it could apply to any business, not mine specifically." [paste the bad response here]
Common things to flag as bad:
  • "This response gave bullet points when I needed flowing prose"
  • "This response hedged every recommendation with caveats"
  • "This response was too shallow — it summarised instead of analysing"
  • "This response gave me the textbook answer, not a specific one for my context"
No bad example yet? Describe the most common failure mode for your task type. For example: "Generic advice that ignores the specific constraints I listed."

📋 Add Domain-Specific Rules +1
What this does: The tool already bans generic bad patterns (hedging, summaries, cheerleading). Your domain has specific ones it doesn't know about. This is where you add them.

How to write them:
  • Start each rule with Do not
  • Be specific — name the exact thing to avoid, not a vague category
  • One rule per line
  • 3–7 rules is optimal. More than 10 dilutes effectiveness.
Examples by domain:
Software engineeringDo not suggest architecture patterns without explaining the trade-offs. Do not recommend libraries without noting maintenance status and bundle size. Do not assume the existing codebase follows any particular pattern. Business strategyDo not use market size estimates without stating the source and year. Do not recommend a pivot unless at least two core assumptions have failed. Do not present a single path forward — always show at least two options. Marketing copyDo not use passive voice in headlines. Do not write benefit statements that could apply to any competitor. Do not include more than one call to action per piece. Research / analysisDo not present contested claims as settled facts. Do not cite secondary sources when primary sources are available. Do not omit contradicting evidence.

🤖 Calibrate for Your AI Model +1
What this does: Each flagship model has different default behaviours. This tool is model-agnostic, which means it's optimally tuned for none of them. Selecting your model applies known fixes automatically.

What each calibration fixes:
  • Claude (Opus 4.8 / Sonnet 4.6) — Tends to be verbose in acknowledgments and adds unnecessary caveats. Calibration: keeps replies brief, blocks apology openers, removes disclaimer habits.
  • GPT-5.5 — Documented improvement in plan calibration (less likely to proceed confidently with a flawed plan) and built for long-horizon agentic work. Calibration: treats self-flagged weaknesses as reliable signal, preserves full multi-step context, blocks auto-formatting.
  • Gemini 3.1 Pro — Independent testing found a measurably higher hallucination rate than comparable models, plus a lower max output length. Calibration: stricter CoVe thresholds (LIKELY→SPECULATIVE, SPECULATIVE→UNKNOWN), front-loads high-value content, re-anchors constraints every turn.
Honesty note: the Gemini hallucination-rate finding is from third-party benchmarking. The Claude and GPT-5.5 behavioural notes are reasoned from public model documentation, not independently measured against this specific prompt set — treat them as informed defaults, not proven facts, and adjust if your own sessions show otherwise.
